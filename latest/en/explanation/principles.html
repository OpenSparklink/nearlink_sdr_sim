Physical Layer Principles¶

This section explains the mathematical principles of key signal processing modules in the SparkLink SLE physical layer.

Polar Coding¶

Channel Polarization¶

Polar codes exploit channel polarization: after applying a linear transform to \(N\) independent channel instances, the sub-channels diverge in reliability \u2014 some approach capacity 1, others approach 0. Arikan proved that this polarization is complete as \(N \to \infty\).

The core transform matrix is the Kronecker power:

\[\begin{split} G_N = B_N \cdot F^{\otimes n}, \quad F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad N = 2^n \end{split}\]

where \(B_N\) is the bit-reversal permutation matrix. The encoding process:

\[ x = u \cdot G_N \]

where the frozen bits of \(u\) (low-reliability sub-channels) are set to 0, and information bits are placed on high-reliability sub-channels.

Reliability Sequence¶

Section 6.9.1.4 of the SparkLink SLE standard defines a fixed reliability ordering table that ranks each sub-channel. The encoder selects the \(K\) most reliable positions for information bits (determined by rate \(R = K/N\)); the remaining positions are filled with known frozen bits.

SC Decoding¶

The Successive Cancellation (SC) decoder makes bit-by-bit decisions in natural order. For bit \(i\):

  • If \(i\) is a frozen bit, it is directly decided as 0.

  • If \(i\) is an information bit, make a hard decision based on the LLR sign: \(\hat{u}_i = \begin{cases} 0, & L_i \geq 0 \\ 1, & L_i < 0 \end{cases}\).

LLR recursive computation relies on two basic operations:

\[ f(a, b) = 2 \tanh^{-1}\!\bigl(\tanh(a/2) \cdot \tanh(b/2)\bigr) \approx \operatorname{sign}(a) \cdot \operatorname{sign}(b) \cdot \min(|a|, |b|) \]
\[ g(a, b, s) = (-1)^s \cdot a + b \]

where \(a, b\) are LLRs from child nodes, and \(s\) is the already-decided upper-level bit.

PSK Modulation¶

Constellation Mapping¶

SparkLink SLE supports three modulation schemes: BPSK, QPSK, and 8PSK. Constellation points are defined as:

\[ s_k = e^{j 2\pi k / M}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, M-1 \]

where \(M \in \{2, 4, 8\}\). The bit-to-symbol mapping uses Gray coding, so adjacent constellation points differ by only 1 bit, reducing the bit error rate at high SNR.

RRC Pulse Shaping¶

The Root Raised Cosine (RRC) filter’s frequency-domain response is:

\[\begin{split} H(f) = \begin{cases} 1, & |f| \leq \frac{1-\alpha}{2T} \\ \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi T}{2\alpha}\left(|f| - \frac{1-\alpha}{2T}\right)\right), & \frac{1-\alpha}{2T} < |f| \leq \frac{1+\alpha}{2T} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{split}\]

where \(\alpha\) is the roll-off factor and \(T\) is the symbol period. An RRC filter at each end cascades to an equivalent raised-cosine filter satisfying the Nyquist ISI-free criterion. The standard specifies \(\alpha = 0.5\) and \(L = 4\) samples per symbol.

GFSK Modulation¶

The instantaneous frequency deviation of Gaussian frequency shift keying is:

\[ f(t) = h \cdot \sum_k a_k \cdot g(t - kT) \]

where \(h\) is the modulation index, \(a_k \in \{-1, +1\}\) are data symbols, and \(g(t)\) is the Gaussian pulse (bandwidth-time product \(BT\)):

\[ g(t) = \frac{1}{2T}\left[\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\ln 2}} \cdot BT \cdot \frac{t + T/2}{T}\right) - \operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{\ln 2}} \cdot BT \cdot \frac{t - T/2}{T}\right)\right] \]

The SparkLink SLE standard specifies modulation index \(h = 0.5\) (minimum shift), \(BT = 0.5\).

Channel Models¶

AWGN¶

Additive White Gaussian Noise channel:

\[ y = x + n, \quad n \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, \sigma^2) \]

The relationship between noise variance and SNR: \(\sigma^2 = P_s / \text{SNR}_{\text{linear}}\), where \(P_s\) is the average signal power.

Rayleigh Fading¶

Flat Rayleigh fading model (no line-of-sight path):

\[ y = h \cdot x + n, \quad h \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, 1) \]

\(|h|\) follows a Rayleigh distribution. In the quasi-static model, the same fading coefficient is used for the entire frame.

Rician Fading¶

Fading model with a line-of-sight path; the K-factor is defined as the ratio of LOS power to scattered power:

\[ h = \sqrt{\frac{K}{K+1}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{K+1}} \cdot h_{\text{scatter}}, \quad h_{\text{scatter}} \sim \mathcal{CN}(0, 1) \]

A larger \(K\) indicates a stronger line-of-sight path. \(K \to \infty\) degenerates to AWGN; \(K = 0\) degenerates to Rayleigh.

Multipath Frequency Selectivity¶

Multipath channels are described using a finite-length FIR model:

\[ y(t) = \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} h_l(t) \cdot x(t - \tau_l) + n(t) \]

where \(\{h_l, \tau_l\}\) are determined by the power delay profile (PDP). Frequency-selective fading causes inter-symbol interference (ISI), which requires an equalizer to compensate.

Doppler Fading and Jakes Model¶

In mobile communications, relative motion between transmitter and receiver causes Doppler frequency shifts, making channel coefficients time-varying. The maximum Doppler shift is:

\[ f_d = \frac{v \cdot f_c}{c} \]

where \(v\) is the relative velocity, \(f_c\) is the carrier frequency, and \(c\) is the speed of light.

The Clarke model states that in an isotropic scattering environment, the autocorrelation function of the time-varying channel is the zeroth-order Bessel function of the first kind:

\[ R(\Delta t) = J_0(2\pi f_d \Delta t) \]

Implementation uses the Jakes sum-of-sinusoids model. Superposition of \(N\) oscillators constructs the time-varying fading coefficient:

\[ h(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{n=1}^{N} e^{j(2\pi f_d \cos\alpha_n \cdot t + \theta_n)} \]

where arrival angles \(\alpha_n\) are uniformly distributed over \([0, 2\pi)\) and initial phases \(\theta_n\) are random. As \(N\) grows large, the statistics approach the true Clarke model:

  • Real and imaginary parts are approximately independent Gaussian

  • Envelope approximately follows Rayleigh distribution

  • The autocorrelation function approaches \(J_0(2\pi f_d \Delta t)\)

For Rician channels, the scattered component uses the Jakes model; the LOS path adds a fixed phase:

\[ h(t) = \sqrt{\frac{K}{K+1}} \cdot e^{j 2\pi f_d t} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{K+1}} \cdot h_\text{Jakes}(t) \]

When \(f_d = 0\), it degenerates to quasi-static fading (constant coefficient per frame); when \(f_d\) is large, the channel varies rapidly within a frame, posing a serious challenge to equalizer and decoder.

Multi-User Interference Model¶

Multi-user interference (MUI) is modeled as an external noise source superimposed on the target signal. The signal-to-interference ratio for the \(i\)-th interferer is defined as:

\[ \text{SIR}_i = \frac{P_\text{signal}}{P_{\text{interferer},i}} \]

Combined signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio computation:

\[ \text{SINR} = \frac{P_\text{signal}}{P_\text{noise} + \sum_{i=1}^{M} P_{\text{interferer},i}} \]

Adjacent-channel interference also requires consideration of frequency offset. The \(i\)-th interfering signal is frequency-shifted and then superimposed:

\[ y(t) = s(t) + \sum_{i=1}^{M} \sqrt{\frac{P_s}{\text{SIR}_i^\text{linear}}} \cdot z_i(t) \cdot e^{j 2\pi \Delta f_i t} + n(t) \]

where \(z_i(t)\) is a normalized-power random interference baseband signal, and \(\Delta f_i\) is the frequency offset between the interferer and the desired signal. \(\Delta f_i = 0\) is co-channel interference; \(\Delta f_i \neq 0\) is adjacent-channel interference, which the receive filter partially attenuates.

Equalizer¶

ZF Equalization¶

Zero-forcing equalization directly inverts in the frequency domain:

\[ \hat{X}(k) = \frac{Y(k)}{H(k)} \]

The advantage is complete ISI elimination; the disadvantage is noise amplification at channel nulls.

MMSE Equalization¶

The minimum mean square error criterion balances ISI elimination against noise suppression:

\[ \hat{X}(k) = \frac{H^*(k)}{|H(k)|^2 + \sigma_n^2 / \sigma_x^2} \cdot Y(k) \]

At high SNR it approaches ZF; at low SNR it automatically reduces gain to avoid noise amplification.

1-Tap Equalization¶

For flat fading channels, the channel coefficient is a scalar \(h\), and MMSE equalization degenerates to a single tap:

\[ \hat{x} = \frac{h^*}{|h|^2 + \sigma^2} \cdot y \]

Code Block Segmentation¶

When the data length exceeds a single Polar code block capacity, the data must be split into multiple code blocks:

  • segment_without_crc (FT2): Splits data according to Polar code block size \(N\) and information bit count \(K\); each segment is encoded independently.

  • segment_with_crc (FT3/FT4): Each segment has a small CRC appended, providing independent error detection per segment.

After splitting, each segment is independently Polar-encoded, rate-matched, and modulated; the receiver processes them in reverse order.

Scrambling¶

Channel bit scrambling uses a Galois linear feedback shift register (LFSR) to generate a pseudo-random sequence XOR-ed with encoded bits:

\[ c_{\text{scrambled}}(i) = c(i) \oplus s(i) \]

Scrambling disperses long runs of 0s and 1s, improving the spectral properties and timing recovery. Descrambling regenerates the same sequence from the same initial seed to recover the original bits.
